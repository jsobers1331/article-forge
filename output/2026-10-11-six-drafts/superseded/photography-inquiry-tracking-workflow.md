# How to track photography inquiries from first message to booking

*Last updated: October 2026.*

Track a photography inquiry as a record with a person, a requested session, a current stage, and one next action. That gives you a reliable answer to “who needs a reply?” without rereading every inbox thread. The system can be a spreadsheet for a small studio or a CRM when several bookings are moving at once. The important habit is to update the record when the client responds, a proposal goes out, or a date is secured.

## What should you capture when an inquiry arrives?

Collect enough information to give a useful reply, without turning the first form into an interrogation. Name, preferred contact method, session type, date or date range, location, and a short description of what the client wants are usually a workable starting point. Ask only for information you can act on at this stage.

Store the inquiry date and its source if the client provided it. This lets you see what is waiting and where inquiries come from, but do not claim a marketing channel caused a booking without attribution you can actually support. ## Which stages make the pipeline useful?

Use stages that describe what happened, not how likely you feel the person is to book. A simple pipeline might be:

| Stage | Evidence that the inquiry belongs here | Next action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New | Message received, no substantive reply sent | Read and respond |
| Replied | You sent a relevant answer or availability check | Wait for or schedule a response |
| Consult or proposal | A call is planned or a scoped offer was sent | Record the decision date |
| Contract sent | The agreement is with the client | Check whether terms need clarification |
| Booked | Your agreed booking conditions are met | Prepare the session |
| Closed | Client declined, date passed, or inquiry was unsuitable | Record the reason if known |

Your studio may use different stage names. Keep the definitions concrete enough that another team member would place the same inquiry in the same stage. “Hot lead” is a judgment; “contract sent” is an event you can check.

## What should the next action contain?

Every open record needs an owner and a next step with a date. “Follow up” alone is too vague. “Send the wedding collection link by Thursday” or “check whether the client chose a consultation time” gives you a task you can finish.

This is where the pipeline prevents dropped inquiries. A client who asked for pricing and never received the correct collection information is different from someone who has the proposal but needs time to decide. The first needs a reply; the second may need space. The [client onboarding guide](/blog/photography-client-onboarding-workflow) explains the later booking stages in more detail.

## How do you follow up without sounding automated?

Refer to the client's specific question and offer one clear next step. If the request is missing a date, ask for it. If the date is unavailable, say so promptly and, when helpful, suggest a narrower alternative you can actually offer. A generic sequence sent regardless of the conversation makes the studio look inattentive.

A practical workflow is:

1. Read the original inquiry and check availability before replying.
2. Answer the actual question, then request only the missing booking details.
3. Record what was sent, the next step, and who owns it.
4. Review open inquiries at a set time each workday or workweek.
5. Stop or change follow-up when the client replies, declines, or asks for space.

## When is a spreadsheet enough?

A spreadsheet can work well if one person handles a small number of inquiries and consistently updates it. It is easy to inspect and export. Its weak point is the manual transfer between the inbox, booking agreement, invoice, and later gallery delivery. A connected system becomes more useful when that transfer causes repeated mistakes or when more than one person needs the same view.

Do not migrate because a software feature list is long. Try one real workflow: capture an inquiry, send a proposal, record a decision, and find the client record a month later.
