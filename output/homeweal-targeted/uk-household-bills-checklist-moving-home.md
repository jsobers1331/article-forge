# UK Household Bills Checklist: Moving Home

*Last updated: August 2026.*

Moving into a new UK home means setting up a fresh set of household bills — council tax, energy, water, broadband, and more — often within a narrow window around move-in day. This checklist walks through every bill you need to handle, who to contact, and how to track it all without losing your place. ## What bills do I need to set up when moving house in the UK?

The core set of UK household bills breaks down into five categories: council tax, energy (electricity and gas), water and sewerage, broadband and TV, and insurance. There is also a smaller set of optional but common bills — a TV licence, home contents insurance, and any subscription services you want to keep.

Here is the full list with who to contact:

| Bill | Who to contact | When to do it |
|---|---|---|
| Council tax | Your local authority | Tell them your move-in date as soon as you know it |
| Electricity and gas | Your chosen supplier (or the existing one) | On or before move-in day; take meter readings |
| Water and sewerage | Your regional water company | Within a few days of moving in |
| Broadband and home phone | Your chosen provider | Book installation 2–4 weeks ahead if possible |
| TV licence | TV Licensing | Immediately if you plan to watch live TV or use iPlayer |
| Contents insurance | Any insurer | Before you move, so your belongings are covered in transit |
| Building insurance | Your mortgage lender or insurer | Before completion if you're buying |

The order matters less than the timing. Council tax and energy are the two that carry penalties if you delay, so prioritise those on move-in day itself.

## How do I sort out energy when moving into a new home?

Energy is the bill most people get wrong, usually because they assume the previous occupant's supplier is locked in. It isn't. According to [Ofgem's guidance on moving home](https://www.ofgem.gov.uk/get-energy-if-you-are-moving-home-or-business-premises), you can choose a new tariff after you move in — you are not stuck with whatever the previous occupant had.

The practical steps are straightforward:

1. **Take meter readings on move-in day** — photograph both electricity and gas meters the moment you get the keys. This protects you from being charged for the previous occupant's usage.
2. **Contact the existing supplier** — tell them you've moved in, give them your readings, and ask them to set up an account in your name. They are obliged to supply you for a short period while you shop around.
3. **Check whether the landlord pays energy bills** — Ofgem's guidance specifically notes that tenants should check this. If your tenancy agreement says bills are included, you don't need to do anything.
4. **Compare and switch** — once you know who the current supplier is, compare tariffs and switch if you find a better deal. The switch typically takes a few weeks, and your new supplier handles most of it.
5. **Set up a payment method** — direct debit is usually cheapest, but you can also pay by cash, card, or prepayment meter depending on your circumstances.

One thing to note: if the property has a prepayment meter, you may need to top it up immediately to have power while you sort out the account. Keep that in mind for your first evening in the house.

## What about council tax — do I need to tell the council I've moved?

Yes, and this is the bill people most often forget until a reminder letter arrives months later. Council tax is administered by local authorities, and [GOV.UK's guidance on council tax when moving home](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/your-property-and-council-tax) is clear that you must tell the local authority when you move.

The process is simple:

- Find your local council's website (they all have a "moving home" section).
- Report your move-in date and the property address.
- Give them your previous address so they can close that account.
- Ask about any discounts you might qualify for — single-person discount (25% off), student exemptions, or council tax reduction schemes.

If you're renting, check your tenancy agreement first. Some landlords include council tax in the rent, in which case you just need to confirm the landlord has notified the council. If you're buying, the solicitor usually handles the notification as part of the conveyancing process, but it's worth confirming this rather than assuming.

Council tax is paid in ten monthly instalments by default, with February and March free. You can ask to spread it over twelve months instead if that suits your cashflow better.

## What about seasonal bills and one-off moving costs?

Most people focus on the monthly bills and forget the ones that arrive twice a year or annually. Water bills in the UK are typically billed every six months. A TV licence is annual. Contents insurance might be annual or monthly depending on how you set it up. And then there are the one-off costs of moving itself — the van hire, the removal company, the new furniture, the first food shop.

A good tracker handles these differently. Seasonal bills should not distort your monthly view of household spending, which is why HomeWeal treats them as a separate category rather than lumping them into a single monthly average. You still see them coming, but they don't make March look like a disaster month when it's just your water bill landing.

For the one-off moving costs, those aren't recurring bills at all — they're a separate budget line. Our advice is to keep them out of your bill tracker entirely and track them in a simple spreadsheet or notes app instead. Mixing one-off costs with recurring bills makes it harder to see your true monthly baseline.

## Who is this checklist not for?

This checklist assumes you're moving into a property where you're responsible for the bills. That's not everyone. If any of the following apply, parts of this guide won't be relevant:

- **Fully furnished rentals with bills included** — some student lets and serviced apartments bundle everything into the rent. You only need to worry about broadband and your own contents insurance.
- **New-build properties** — the developer sometimes pays the energy bills for a period, and council tax may be reduced or waived for the first few months. Check your purchase or tenancy paperwork.
- **Homes with a managing agent** — some leasehold properties have the managing agent handle communal bills (water for communal areas, building insurance). You still need your own energy and council tax, but the agent handles the rest.

If you're in one of these situations, the checklist still works — just cross off the bills you don't need to worry about.

## What are the most common mistakes people make?

Three mistakes come up again and again, and they're all avoidable:

**Forgetting to take meter readings.** This is the single most common error. If you don't record your readings on move-in day, the previous occupant's usage gets attributed to you, and you'll spend weeks on the phone disputing a bill. Photograph the meters, note the readings in your tracker, and send them to the supplier on day one.

**Not telling the council quickly enough.** Council tax is backdated to your move-in date, so delaying notification doesn't save you money — it just means a larger bill later. Some councils also charge a fine for late notification, though this varies.

**Setting up direct debits before you know the amounts.** If you estimate too low, you'll get a catch-up bill. If you estimate too high, you're giving the supplier an interest-free loan. Wait until you have at least one actual bill before setting up a direct debit, or use a tracker that lets you estimate and then confirm, as we described earlier.
